Curatorial Statement

Etching is the effort, or the consequence, of selectively removing specified matter from a surface so that a durable trace remains. As a geological undertaking, this technique expands and recurs across the scales of chips, trenches, and mines. The question is never merely functional; it always entails a judgment of value. Rubble and waste deemed in urgent need of removal must first be named before one can determine which strata, treasures, and patterns are to be kept. In its more violent—if not most violent—form, this extractive geology turns strata into cross-sections. Drilling and hydraulic fracturing expose buried energy to the daylight of exchange. At the same time, data inevitably settle into dim granite caverns as new sedimentary layers, while emptied salt beds and mineral veins return the favor through the abrupt collapse of sinkholes, giving measurable territory back as an immeasurable abyss.

This almost paranoid stripping-away eventually seems to approach a limit at which the very ground on which humans stand becomes a vanishing thing. Under such conditions, the earth refuses to remain a silent support and responds through depletion, collapse, and regression. The irony is nearly too exact: people excavate and drill with their attention fixed downward, only to hollow out the ground from which leverage might be gained. The not-yet-ruined stairway available for climbing becomes the last remaining hope. The determination to confront the earth consequently assumes the form of an impulse to take flight.

The posture of wingsuit flying remains profoundly ambiguous, suspended across several tensions that pull against one another in different forms. It declares an almost terrifying embodiment through bodily risk while openly depending on technology as prosthesis. It projects the visual sovereignty of a bird's-eye view, attempting to reduce the ground to a flattened surface of things, while the rapid loss of altitude makes vertical space—and its ferocity and uncontrollability—the very matter of the experience.

Wingsuit flying is also a stunt: a spectacle that appears to do too much while, in fact, doing too little, wavering in its own excess. This equivocal bodily technique and aesthetic device readily elevates the earth into a magnificent sublime, yet it also renders the earth as an operable and desirable blueprint, even one that actively solicits development—an engineered Goddess of Mount Wu. It celebrates, and at the same time legitimates, a particular operation of administrative–real-estate capital. On a broader historical scale, we know only too well that the possibility of this operation has always rested on the earth's debasement, cheapening, objectification, and extensive dispossession.

Yet the intimacy among body, technology, and earth intimated by wingsuit flying exceeds this deployment. Its errant energies suggest the possibility of a non-exploitative relation. What ethics might emerge when the risk and fear of falling are no longer translated into a speculative model, but are recognized as the ground of human dependence on the earth? When vulnerability and injurability unfold through the burning friction of air and the approach of precipitous terrain, how might the enduring fall of human collectivities—or of the human species—toward deep time and planetary disaster be recognized anew, and thereby reimagined?

In the end, rapid descent may puncture the desire to turn the earth into a blueprint. When the lament and scream of an extracted, etched, and collapsing planet become a sharp whistle, those things withdrawing from the human demand a fatal encounter.

Text by Juntao Yang

About the Artists

Shi Qing

Born in 1969, Shi Qing lives and works in Shanghai. His practice moves across installation, moving image, performance, and their combinations, emphasizing discontinuous advances in subjective practice and media writing grounded in fieldwork. An active organizer of social and collective projects, he is a co-founder of Radical Space, Chongqing Work Institute, and Jiangnan Stack, and has initiated projects including Collectivist Architecture, Local Work, Hinterland Project, and Shanghai Film Geography.

Yu Guo

Yu Guo is an artist who graduated from the Oil Painting Department of Sichuan Fine Arts Institute in 2016 and now lives and works in Chongqing. His practice spans painting, moving image, and writing; recent works explore the interweaving of image and text.

Sasha Litvintseva

Sasha Litvintseva is a London-based artist, filmmaker, and writer. Since 2018, much of her work has been made through an ongoing collaboration with Beny Wagner. Her films have screened at festivals internationally and her work has been exhibited by major art institutions. She is Senior Lecturer in Film at Queen Mary University of London and holds a PhD in Media, Communications and Cultural Studies from Goldsmiths; her first degree was in Fine Art at the Slade School of Fine Art. Her writing has appeared in books and journals.

Emma Charles

Emma Charles is an artist working with film, photography, and sound. She studied photography at the Royal College of Art and later attended the National Film and Television School. Her work explores different forms of invisibility and is drawn to subjects that remain concealed, hidden, or out of reach, from the physical spaces of otherwise invisible technologies to more ethereal concepts of the unseen. In recent years, her practice has examined the intersections of technology and geology through rare-earth minerals, media archaeology, deep time, and nuclear culture.

About the Curator

Juntao Yang

Juntao Yang is a writer, artist, and curator. His research, writing, and artistic practice attend to traces of micropower within specific sites of visual and material culture; visible and invisible conflicts and violence within everyday conditions of survival; and the organization and mutation of power's agents. His interests include planetarity and geological thought, disaster media and ontology, and the ethics of survival. He is a PhD student in Film and Media at the University of California, Berkeley, and previously earned degrees from Wuhan University and Columbia University.
