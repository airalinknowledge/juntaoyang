Dallas: Techniques of Anti-Urbanism

Dallas reveals how business oligarchy, suburbanization, pedestrian tunnels, and speculative towers hollow the city while repeatedly claiming to revive it.

Following I-35 north—the cutting line that, as I mentioned earlier, divides two worlds—the railway reaches Dallas. This may be the most tedious stretch of the Texas Eagle’s view: an endless gray-brown plain, almost entirely without even the mines, refineries, and flour mills that occasionally interrupt the landscape farther south. This sense of purposelessness reveals one side of Dallas. The city seems to exist for no reason, or at least for no necessity. Dallas rose at the junction of the Houston and Texas Central and the Texas and Pacific railways. Its early leaders created that crossroads through payment and legislative maneuver, by almost any means available. The oligarchic rule of commercial elites that took shape around this act of infrastructural fabrication has persisted, in altered form, into the present.

Businessmen excel at extracting profit but seldom at sustaining a city. Or perhaps the city, from their point of view, is difficult to manage and therefore difficult to monetize. For all their considerable differences, the visions associated with Ebenezer Howard and Daniel Burnham—and the villainous version of Le Corbusier reconstructed by Jane Jacobs—share an impulse to purify the city of what appears dirty, disorderly, dangerous, ugly, and overcrowded: the promiscuous mixture of crowds, vendors, vagrants, and souvenirs. Dallas, too, has been deliberately forged into a hollow city. Its population was engineered toward secluded suburbs with enclosed lots, long blocks, and rigid functional zoning, then made to cross a grandiose, hostile, and terrifying freeway network by private car. I stayed across from the overgrown parkland along the Trinity River and once tried to cross one of its interchanges on foot. The pedestrian strip was so narrow that speeding cars seemed to brush my arm.

Through deliberate channeling and segregation, the city was emptied out. Housing disappeared first, followed by restaurants and entertainment, until little remained beyond municipal institutions and office towers. By 2026, even those last office towers stand vacant on a massive scale. The Dallas central business district has an office vacancy rate of roughly one third. The city seems to be approaching something like death.

Dallas elites made several attempts to rescue downtown during the long second half of the twentieth century, though their sincerity remains doubtful. Their strategy might be called the suburbanization of the city. One technique was enclosure. Around I. M. Pei’s City Hall, an immense civic plaza and other public facilities define an expanse that is almost entirely without people. If, like me, you make a point of walking there, you may find yourself the only pedestrian among vacant parking lots and spaces that seem to belong exclusively to the Texas sun. To the north, the Arts District pursued a more explicitly gentrifying version of the same operation, yet buildings designed around luxury hotels and restaurants remain strangely quiet.

The most sustained and radical campaign of renewal, however, may be Thanks-Giving Square and its surroundings. The area offers a compressed history of how postwar optimism imagined the city. The Republic Center opened in 1954, its rocket-like spire thrust vertically toward the sky at the beginning of a thirty-year compact between Space Age futurism and capitalist optimism. The interfaith Chapel of Thanks-Giving, completed in 1976, twists upward in a spiral; its stained glass, hand-cut and set in epoxy resin, promises a universal faith capable of escaping the shadow of JFK’s assassination. Santander Tower, completed during the 1980s oil boom, adds another stratum. Financial oligarchs built large numbers of late-modern and Postmodern office towers in the name of urban revival, though their operative purpose was real-estate speculation.

Philip Johnson and John Burgee’s Comerica Bank Tower is exemplary. Its exaggerated barrel vault, seized from Renaissance architectural tradition, replaces masonry with a vast glass curtain wall; below its banking hall lies an orderly field of office cubicles. This almost wasteful grandeur served above all to project financial optimism and the heroic self-image of the corporation. The building opened in 1987 as Momentum Place for MCorp, only for the bank to collapse soon afterward. Its heroic image was therefore shadowed from the beginning by foreclosure and the exhaustion of the oil economy that had produced it.

The more interesting remains lie in less perceptible strata. The most utopian legacy of the 1970s urban-renewal movement hides beneath plazas and buildings. One level consists of the Dallas Pedestrian Network: enclosed passages connecting parking garages, offices, and shopping concourses while shielding their users from the weather. Narrow, low, and sealed corridors were designed for rapid movement, yet they also solicited restaurants and retail. The apparent contradiction served a coherent end. White-collar workers arriving by car from the suburbs could move from garage to office without setting foot on a city street. The system was anti-urban at its core because it imagined the street as something to be avoided.

Beneath the pedestrian concourse lies the ambitious Bullington Truck Terminal. Excavated to a depth of fifty-five feet, the two-level complex was intended to consolidate street-level delivery traffic and connect directly to four surrounding buildings. Its logistical machinery has not entirely ceased to operate: recent city documents still describe the terminal as essential to loading and unloading for its surface users. Its public life, however, has effectively vanished. I searched for every entrance to this legendary subterranean suburb and found them all locked, even as the lights remained on. The distinction is decisive. Access disappears while maintenance continues; the city is withdrawn, but its service infrastructure persists.

Dallas is a cost and a warning. Deadening anti-urban planning does not generate renewal. The grand designs of the 1970s continued to erase urban life, while the skyline produced by 1980s oil finance survives as vacant speculative property. A long decline followed. The planners’ utopian fixations and the oligarchs’ designs combine into a peculiar temporal perversion: more than a cityscape frozen in the 1980s, it is a layered machinery in which successive futures remain active after their promises have failed. In a public artwork downtown, Alicia Eggert’s neon sign announces: “NOW IS ALWAYS THE TIME FOR BECOMING.”
