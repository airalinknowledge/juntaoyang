The Innocence of the West, Colonial Debt, and Rick’s Return

Rick’s pastoral vacation exposes the Western as a machine that converts settler colonialism into rural innocence, even as the series restores the melancholic antihero its audience cannot bear to lose.

Having severed his memory of his own history and of the relationships that bind him to the present, Rick assumes the name Ted and settles into an archetypal Western rural milieu: craft, land, cheap amusements, the kindness of ordinary people, and a moral world that, for the moment, can still be trusted and felt to be safe. He calls it a vacation. Like a serious Western, the plot quickly becomes a story of sincere rural whites and their harmonious, simple social relations attempting to resist the predations of abstract urban culture and metropolitan capitalism upon an embodied way of life. Yet the pressure is spelled out throughout by Rick’s hologram: this rural refuge has never been more than a moral outside continually fabricated by urban subjects, then used as an emotional infrastructure for escaping pressure, responsibility, and pain.

But the problem lies not only in the urban white subject’s manipulation and exploitation of the countryside. It also lies in the way this separation, on a larger historical scale, erases history itself. If the former can be called a kind of internal colonization, naming it as such obscures the latter’s naked historical coloniality; the viewer must read the two as mutually implicating figures emerging from this repression. The Western performs a kind of leisure-time transposition: it moralizes and victimizes the local communities of early capitalism—free-ranging cattlemen, settlers, small farmers, and artisans—in order to indict a later, more concentrated and less disguised phase of capitalist colonial aggression: the railroad, the bank, the property developer, the chain supermarket. The capitalist invaders who arrive later thus retroactively transform rural whites, those earlier colonizers, into natives, evacuating their colonial debt to people of color. Urban viewers enjoy the separation produced by rural aesthetics because it absolves them of historical responsibility and allows them to reinvest in the sincere, courageous, innocent subject. We watch an upright rural white man defend women, land, and community in a finite and legible conflict; when the film ends, we return to lives structured by capital, colonial history, and gendered responsibility, consoled that the moral world can still be understood. Rick’s vacation is therefore also the vacation the Western offers its audience.

Season 9, Episode 2 handles these two structures of oppression—one inside the fiction, the other outside it—with extraordinary intelligence, because Ted and Rick are in fact the same person, occupying two temporal stages of the colonial process. The innocent rural white subject can therefore no longer be separated from the colonial history that produced him. When Rick’s machines begin attacking the bowling alley, Ted rapidly becomes the Western hero defending his home, mobilizing local craft and communal solidarity against a high-tech invasion. Yet although urban technological power does indeed colonize the countryside, the white man who claims to defend authenticity has carried that power within him all along. In the episode’s second half, the world he has cast aside—a world dense with relationships, conflicts, responsibilities, and debts—repeatedly demands his return. However fiercely he resists, as concrete people, things, and ghosts surround him, pleading with and commanding him to take responsibility, the white man is almost forced to relearn what he should never have been permitted to forget. Ironically, he also reconstitutes relations of power and destroys those apparently innocent rural subjects. By the time Ted commands Rick’s power, he has already repeated Rick so completely that whether the undramatic red button actually works becomes irrelevant. Perhaps Ted dies the moment he recognizes that he must return to responsibility. The button no longer produces Rick’s return; it registers the instant of pressing as confirmation that the return has already occurred. Yet although the acknowledgment of responsibility carries ethical weight, debt is not redeemed simply because a path to self-knowledge has opened. The devastation is merely left in place—no longer concealed, but no more repaired—and the episode offers no guarantee that Rick will alter the conditions of power that continually produce harm, much less become a more just person.

Perhaps the episode’s most ingenious move is that, after spending nearly twenty minutes on the ethical burden of the rural fantasy, its ending uses the slightest phrase—“It did”—to recall the full dramatic weight of Rick as a character. Theoretical critique does not diminish his allure; instead, it produces still more wounds in which the audience can invest, summoning once again the battered man of infinite competence: sorrowful, fragile to the point of collapse, a melancholic god incapable of recovering what he has lost. This is the central melodramatic appeal of Rick and Morty. Rick seems capable of solving nearly every external problem, yet he cannot restore the losses that truly constitute him. The closer his power comes to divinity, the more cruelly irreversible those losses become. Tragedy arises from the fact that even after power has expanded without limit, it still cannot bring back the dead, make the injury unhappen, or allow him to become the person who never caused it.

An ego possessed by the ghosts of what it has lost is a mechanism contemporary prestige television has mastered in its antihero narratives: the harder a character is to forgive ethically, the more psychological density, performative space, and intimacy with the audience the text grants him. The accumulated memory and duration of serial television allow the audience’s attachment to exceed the moral judgment of any single action, while trauma, loss, and occasional flashes of need and vulnerability become points of entry through which viewers may invest the character with sublimity. Yet this emotional alchemy often conflates the character’s own irredeemability with the irreversible losses he has inflicted on others. Both forms of loss are routed into the same melancholic subject, transmuting guilt into charisma. The most accomplished meta-work of Season 9, Episode 2 is to write this audience desire into the narrative itself. If Ted truly remained in happiness, Rick as the character we know would cease to exist. Morty therefore needs Rick to return, even if he must deceive him; the audience needs Rick to return; the television industry needs Rick to return as well. And so Rick returns: the mind that understands everything, can destroy the world, and is always on the verge of breaking. Mourning is not permitted to come to an end. The red button consequently marks the audience’s cruelty and numbness, too.

Looking back over the episode’s work, it both pays homage to and mocks the Western’s fantasy of innocence. At the level of narrative, it restores Rick and the white colonizer alike as subjects of responsibility; at the level of feeling, it crowns once more the god we know we should not readily forgive but cannot bear to lose. It is the most beautiful structure I can imagine within a twenty-two-minute episode. First, it establishes Ted’s world through an almost banal slowness and warmth. Technological violence then gradually contaminates that rhythm, and the ensuing resistance is temporarily elevated into the myth of the Western hero. After a sequence of science-fiction action, colonial allegory, and philosophical inquiry into personhood, the ending contracts everything into the garage—a device the series often uses. Through Morty’s hesitation, a brief silence, and very few words, a minimalist intimacy of immense industrial appeal is born.
