Naked Wounds and the Abandoned

Digital Photography
2024
Naked Wounds and the Abandoned: It's Been Happening All Along, digital photography, 2024

Introduction

The digital photography series Naked Wounds and the Abandoned is based on the Tangshan Pit Park in Nanjing. This area was once the largest abandoned quarry around Nanjing, the Longquan Quarry. Continuously and heavily mined since the 1970s, Longquan Quarry was gradually shut down and abandoned after the turn of the millennium; in recent years, in response to national policies on environmental protection and development, the area has undergone ecological rehabilitation and development as an eco-park, including luxury Holiday Inn hotels. Due to Tangshan's distance from downtown Nanjing and inconvenient transport, visitor traffic to the pit park is limited. Part of the park is now open to the public, but the area's native forests have almost disappeared, and its ecological situation differs sharply from the well-preserved hills around it, with five huge pits remaining exposed and barren.

Since the Industrial Revolution, humans have proved to be a force for shaping the landscape, with geological materials and fossil energy sources buried deep beneath the earth's surface entangled with, facilitating, or constraining human social and technological progress. This invites a vertical version of geopolitics under the Anthropocene paradigm: the Tangshan pits have moved from collective geological resources in the socialist era to outcomes and consumer goods of neoliberal environmental governance. As a collective geomorphological event, they suggest how we are today situated in a “society made of geology” and owe various “geopolitical and evolutionary debts” to geological materials. Long geological time intersects with everyday human time and the time of capitalist globalisation in the geomorphological event embodied by the pit, which can be seen as both a microcosmic slice of present-day life and social conditions and as their subterranean prehistory, unleashing an alternative geographic imagination.

In the 1990s, local labourers found a well-preserved fossilised skull in construction waste in a karst cave in Tangshan, later identified and named Nanjing Man, thought to be a form of Homo erectus from roughly 58,000 years ago. The discovery unsettled established accounts of human dispersal and became one piece of evidence calling for revisions to standard models of human origins. At the same time, discoveries of East Asian Homo erectus have fuelled folk nationalist attempts to separate ancient Chinese ancestry from European and African lineages through archaeological evidence. The accidental discovery of Nanjing Man also offers a metaphor for the intimate relationship between human fossils and geological materiality, revealing common origin, mutual differentiation, and eventual decomposition and merging. Fossils as objects of knowledge therefore alter and split the taxonomic order of things.

Through digital photography and digital manipulation, including analogue screen-print processes, Naked Wounds and the Abandoned transforms exposed mine surfaces into textures approximating human skin, blurring distinctions between geology and human beings, non-living matter and the living. The works parody the style of popular posters in the neoliberal market, juxtaposing elements with complex connotations in an attempt to stimulate critical reflection.
