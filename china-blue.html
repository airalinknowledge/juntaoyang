Screening Info

China Blue

Saturday, April 30, 2022

School of Arts, Wuhan University, Wuhan, China

Curated by Juntao Yang

Film: Micha X. Peled, China Blue, documentary, 2005

Curatorial Note

China Blue, filmed in China in the early twenty-first century and completed in 2005, enters a blue-jeans factory in Shaxi, Guangdong. Its central figure is Jasmine, a young migrant worker from Sichuan. Working through deep-access observational footage while also drawing on first-person voice-over, subjective imagery, and conspicuous narrative organization, the film follows the routes by which young rural women enter factory labor and the forms of life they sustain across the production line, the dormitory, and their brief intervals of rest.

This screening took the project’s 2002 production start as the point of departure for a twentieth-anniversary retrospective. According to the screening history available to this program, it was also the first complete presentation of the film within China. In 2008, a prominent underground cultural venue in Beijing had planned to screen the documentary during the period of limited cultural opening surrounding the Olympic Games. Although permission had reportedly been granted, plainclothes police cut the electricity and forced the event to disperse.

By the early 2000s, “Made in China” had become a world-historical economic phenomenon. The labor relations, commodity chains, and asymmetrical exchanges concealed beneath that phrase nevertheless demanded renewed scrutiny. Across the subcontracting zones of coastal southeastern China, the question is not only “who manufactures for whom?” We must also ask how the privileged positions occupied by Western investors, brands, and consumers within international capitalism bear concretely on the bodies, time, and life chances of workers in the Global South. China Blue renders these relations—ordinarily concealed by the commodity’s surface—immediate and perceptible.

China Blue as Documentary

China Blue is the second installment of Micha X. Peled’s Globalization Trilogy. The three films unfold along the production–consumption chain: Store Wars: When Wal-Mart Comes to Town examines the conflict generated by Walmart’s arrival in a small American town; China Blue enters China’s export garment industry; and Bitter Seeds turns to Indian cotton farmers, genetically modified seed, and agrarian debt. Through locally specific cases with considerable narrative force, Peled traces how economic globalization operates capillarily, intervening in everyday life across distant regions.

The film appeared at the Toronto International Film Festival and the International Documentary Film Festival Amsterdam in 2005. It received IDFA’s Amnesty Human Rights Award, the PBS Independent Lens Audience Award, and an Award of Excellence from the Society for Visual Anthropology of the American Anthropological Association. These distinctions register the film’s unusual movement across documentary festivals, public television, and visual anthropology.

Dependency theory offers one important set of analytical coordinates for the film, though it does not exhaust the complexity of the relations on screen. The documentary shows how an economic periphery enters commodity chains dominated by transnational brands and pursues export competitiveness through inexpensive labor. The benefits of “dependent development” are distributed unevenly among multinational firms, local governments, factory owners, and workers, while production risks and temporal pressures are continually displaced toward the far end of the supply chain.

Beyond this political-economic analysis, the film possesses an unusual sensorium. Peled interweaves rough, constrained location footage with the private moods of the young women. Handheld cinematography, filming under conditions of restricted access, first-person voice-over, subjective shots, and carefully organized dramatic progression allow the work to move among direct cinema, character study, and activist documentary. The workers do not appear merely as passive victims of globalization; the film remains attentive to friendship, aspiration, humor, and minor forms of resistance.

Micha X. Peled as Activist Filmmaker

Micha X. Peled is an Israeli-born documentary filmmaker who has long lived and worked in San Francisco. After immigrating to the United States, he worked in import trade, freelance journalism, and peace activism, including the Nuclear Freeze movement of the 1980s. These experiences placed transnational commodity circulation, social movements, and media activism at the center of his filmmaking.

Peled served as executive director of the San Francisco media-watch organization Media Alliance and completed his first television documentary, Will My Mother Go Back to Berlin?, in 1992. He subsequently directed Inside God’s Bunker and You, Me, Jerusalem, establishing a body of work concerned with migration, religious conflict, and political community. In 1999, he founded the nonprofit production organization Teddy Bear Films. The Globalization Trilogy would later trace the transnational connections among consumption, manufacturing, and raw materials.
