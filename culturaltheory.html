Hedging Its Bets: Critical Theory and/or Cultural Studies

Treating Cultural Studies and Critical Theory as interchangeable erases the founding antagonism between their accounts of popular culture, agency, and method.

<content>
As a term with a strict intellectual genealogy, Cultural Studies begins with the Birmingham School. When Stuart Hall joined the Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies in 1964 and later became its director, its central concern was the everyday cultural practice of Britain’s postwar working class. Its founding texts understood culture as ordinary, bottom-up, and grounded in lived experience.
This position stood in fundamental opposition to the elitism of the first generation of the Frankfurt School, which treated mass culture as a product of the culture industry and the masses as its passive recipients. When early Cultural Studies scholars criticized Leavisism, were they not also, by implication, criticizing Adorno? The two positions share a structural premise: the masses are manipulable, and cultural decline follows necessarily from industrialization. The Birmingham School—and especially the panoramic expansion of Cultural Studies that followed—established its intellectual identity through a rejection of this simplification. To place Cultural Studies under the umbrella of Critical Theory is to obscure this founding antagonism.
When Hall and John Storey discuss resistance and negotiation, their position is plainly different from that of the Frankfurt theorist. Adorno’s framework leaves little room for popular resistance; even resistance itself can appear as a safety valve already anticipated by the system.
Methodologically, Cultural Studies rejected purely deductive theory and inclined toward ethnographic participation. Many CCCS projects of the 1970s used forms of participant observation and subcultural analysis, most famously in the work of Paul Willis. The Frankfurt School, notwithstanding Benjamin as its celebrated outsider and its own forms of empirical social research, rarely made ethnographic fieldwork central. Its interrogative posture remained predominantly philosophical and speculative.
The American importation of theory may be the chief culprit in conflating the two. During the so-called “theory moment” of the 1980s and 1990s, continental theory was translated on a massive scale, curricularized through institutions on the East Coast, and assembled into graduate seminars. Adorno, Foucault, Derrida, Hall, and Spivak were bound into the same anthologies and taught in the same classrooms. This popularization erased the latent antagonisms among thinkers formed by different intellectual traditions, political situations, and methodological commitments. All were flattened into a canon called “theory,” or “Critical Theory.”
A disciplinary name condenses an intellectual commitment. To treat Cultural Studies and Critical Theory casually as equivalent or interchangeable umbrella terms misreads intellectual history and produces serious strategic errors in actual research. The question is not whether these traditions can enter into dialogue; of course they can. Dialogue depends on recognizing difference before announcing homogeneity.
This is more than a problem of disciplinary knowledge. Extended outward, it touches the real core of the politics of difference today.
